      05 · IMPACT AND FEASIBILITY

      Who it helps, what it costs, where it goes next

      FlameAtlas is built to be used, kept up to date and extended without a budget.

      Who uses it

      • Habitat and vehicle designers compare cabin materials and atmospheres before they commit to costly flammability tests.
      • Mission safety teams check fire response choices: fan shutdown, extinguishing agent, smoke detection timing. F18F14F11
      • Researchers and space agencies use the evidence-gap map to see which experiments are still missing. Gaps
      • Students and the public use the Flame Lab to see why fire behaves differently in space.

      Beyond space

      The smoke detection problem studied on the ISS also applies to closed environments on Earth. NASA's SAME investigation notes that accurate smoke detection in places such as submarines and underwater laboratories can save lives. S29

      The same approach (collect scattered test results, rank them for a scenario, show the gaps) can be reused for any safety field where data are spread across many reports.

      Cost and sustainability

      $0hosting (GitHub Pages)
      0servers or databases
      1file to maintain

      The whole tool is one HTML file with no libraries and no backend. Adding a new NASA result means adding one record with its source. The code and data are open, so others can review and extend them.

      Roadmap

      • NASA PSI data tables: add raw test matrices from BASS-II and other investigations to the flammability map.
      • Lunar data: add results from NASA's FM2 project, which plans the first long-duration burns in lunar gravity. S11
      • Partner agency data: add JAXA's FLARE experiments on Kibo, which study material flammability under gravity effects, including applicability to the lunar surface. S28
      • More materials: extend the atlas to wire insulation and other cabin materials as flight data appear. S25

      08 · METHOD

      How FlameAtlas works

      STEP 1 · INGEST

      Collect

      NASA Physical Sciences Informatics investigation records, NASA Technical Reports Server papers and peer-reviewed follow-ups for each experiment.

      STEP 2 · EXTRACT

      Structure

      We used Claude (an SI tool) to read each NASA paper and investigation record and draft structured records: test conditions (oxygen, pressure, airflow, gravity, material) and outcomes. Team Divalar then checks every number against its source.

      STEP 3 · RANK

      Score

      Each finding gets a mission relevance score, an evidence weight (flight scale beats drop tower beats model) and a safety impact level.

      STEP 4 · INTERPRET

      Explain

      The risk console and the assistant turn the ranked evidence into scenario scores and cited answers, and flag where data is missing.

      WHAT THE SOURCE CHECKS CAUGHT
      • Found by the team: a source link the SI produced (old S2) could not be found. Replaced with a verified BASS-II paper.
      • Found by the team: a "4.5 cm gap" claim was not in its source. Removed and replaced with a verified Saffire V finding (F19).
      • Found in a second source-by-source review the team requested: a Saffire-II acrylic result was a normal-gravity value, not a spaceflight one. Removed from the map.
      • Ribbed-acrylic test conditions corrected to 26.9% O₂ and 76 kPa (F22); silicone smoke mean size corrected to 227 nm (F12).
      • Statements softened where the source said less than the draft (F6, F7, F21).

      This is why FlameAtlas keeps people in the loop: SI makes reading dozens of reports fast, and checking every number against its source keeps the atlas right.

      Limitations: partial-gravity data come from drop towers and aircraft lasting seconds, not long-duration tests. Risk weights are the team's judgment from the cited evidence. Treat scores as a way to compare scenarios and find the evidence, not as a design allowable.
